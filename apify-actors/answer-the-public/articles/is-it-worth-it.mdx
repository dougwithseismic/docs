# Is Answer the Public Worth It? (After Using It for 2 Years)

I'll save you the suspense: For most people, no.

But let me explain how I got there, because the answer depends entirely on what you're trying to do.

## What I Wanted ATP to Do

When I first discovered Answer the Public, I was running SEO for multiple clients. The promise was simple: Find what people actually search for, create content around it, get traffic.

The colorful wheel charts were impressive in client presentations. The data seemed comprehensive. The pricing seemed reasonable compared to enterprise SEO tools.

I signed up for Pro at $99/month.

## What ATP Actually Did

After two years of using it, here's the honest assessment:

### The Good

**Question discovery actually works.** If I search "email marketing," ATP shows me real questions people ask:
- how to start email marketing
- what is email marketing automation
- why email marketing is important

This is genuinely useful. These questions become:
- Blog post topics
- FAQ sections
- Video scripts
- Social media content

One client added ATP questions to their product pages with FAQ schema. Featured snippet traffic increased 34% in 90 days. That's real value.

**The visualizations sell.** When presenting to non-technical stakeholders, the wheel charts work. CMOs understand "here's what your customers are asking" better than raw keyword lists.

I've used ATP visualizations in dozens of client presentations. They work for selling the strategy, even if I don't use them for execution.

### The Bad

**No search volume data.** This is the killer limitation.

ATP shows "email marketing for underwater basket weavers" because it appeared in autocomplete somewhere. But without volumes, you have no idea if it's:
- 10,000 searches/month (worth targeting)
- 10 searches/month (ignore it)

I spent $99/month on ATP and still paid $129/month for Ahrefs just to validate which questions were worth answering.

**No keyword difficulty.** Even if a question has volume, can you rank for it?

ATP doesn't tell you that "email marketing tips" is dominated by DR90+ sites you'll never outrank. You discover that after writing the article and getting zero traffic.

**Manual workflow doesn't scale.** For 100 keywords:
- Search each keyword
- Export CSV
- Clean duplicate data
- Remove nonsense
- Repeat

This took 5-6 hours monthly. At my hourly rate, that's $250-300 in labor costs on top of the $99 subscription.

Real cost: $350-400/month when you count time.

### The Ugly

**The data isn't proprietary.** This took me embarrassingly long to realize.

ATP doesn't have special access to secret keyword data. It's querying the same autocomplete APIs anyone can access. Google's autocomplete, YouTube's autocomplete, etc.

You're paying $99/month for organization and presentation of publicly available data.

Once I understood this, the value proposition shifted dramatically.

## When I Actually Got Value from ATP

Despite the limitations, ATP delivered value in specific scenarios:

### Client Presentations

The visualizations work for selling strategies. Showing a CMO "here's what 10,000 people are asking about your product" is more compelling than a spreadsheet.

I still occasionally use ATP (free tier) just to generate wheel charts for proposals.

### FAQ Content Strategy

ATP questions map perfectly to FAQ schema:
```json
{
  "@type": "Question",
  "name": "How to start email marketing?",
  "acceptedAnswer": { ... }
}
```

Multiple clients saw featured snippet wins by converting ATP questions into structured FAQ sections.

### Finding Content Angles

Sometimes I'd search a keyword and discover angles I hadn't considered:
- "email marketing for nonprofits"
- "email marketing with no budget"
- "email marketing alternatives to Mailchimp"

These became use case pages, comparison content, and budget-specific guides that actually converted.

## Where ATP Failed Me

### Bulk Keyword Research

Client needs content calendar covering 50 topics. With ATP:
- 50 manual searches
- 50 CSV exports
- 3-4 hours of data cleaning
- Still need Ahrefs to validate volumes

This workflow was unsustainable.

### Team Collaboration

ATP is single-user even on Pro. For a team of 3:
- Cost: $297/month
- Coordination: Everyone maintains separate accounts
- Data sharing: Export → Slack → reimport

For an agency with 5 people, that's $495/month with zero collaboration features.

### ROI Tracking

After 8 months at $99/month ($792 total), I tried to calculate ROI:

**Direct value:**
- Featured snippet wins from FAQ content: Measurable positive impact
- Content topic discovery: Valuable but hard to quantify
- Client presentation value: Helpful for sales

**Opportunity cost:**
- Could have spent $792 on 6 months of Ahrefs
- Ahrefs includes everything ATP does plus volumes, difficulty, backlinks, rank tracking
- Or spent $0 and accessed the same data via automation

The math didn't work.

## Is ATP Worth It for You?

After all this, here's my framework:

### ATP is Worth It If:

**You're doing client-facing work where presentations matter**
- The visualizations genuinely help sell strategies
- You bill clients for strategy work
- $99/month is easily recovered in one client deliverable

**You research fewer than 20 keywords monthly**
- Free tier (3/day) might actually cover you
- Or Pro at $99/month if you need them all at once
- Labor savings don't justify automation

**You specifically need the ATP branded interface**
- Some clients recognize and trust the brand
- White-label reports matter to you
- You're willing to pay for the UI/UX

### ATP is NOT Worth It If:

**You need search volume data**
- ATP doesn't provide it
- You'll pay for Ahrefs anyway
- Why pay twice?

**You research at scale (50+ keywords monthly)**
- Manual workflow becomes unsustainable
- Labor costs exceed subscription cost
- Automation makes more sense

**You have a team**
- Per-user pricing kills ROI
- $495/month for 5 people is absurd
- Automation has no user limits

**You want API access**
- Not available on Pro ($99/month)
- Expert tier ($199/month) has limited API
- Automation gives full API from day one
