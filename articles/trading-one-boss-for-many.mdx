## The Freedom Fantasy

<Info>
**What's the most common misconception about leaving employment?**
That you're escaping bosses. In reality, you're trading one boss (who you can learn to manage) for multiple bosses (who all have different expectations, communication styles, and levels of respect for your time).
</Info>

Here's what I thought when I was employed:

"My boss is unreasonable. Deadlines are arbitrary. I don't have control over my work. If I could just work for myself, I'd have freedom."

Here's what I learned after years of client work:

**In a job you have one boss.**
**With clients you have many bosses.**

And the problems I wanted to escape? Most of them followed me.

## What Actually Happens When You Go from Employment to Clients

### Problem: Unreasonable Deadlines

**In employment**: Your manager sets unrealistic deadlines based on business needs you might not fully understand.

**With clients**: Every client sets unrealistic deadlines based on *their* business needs you definitely don't fully understand. And you have 3-5 of them doing this simultaneously.

**The difference**: Instead of negotiating with one manager who you can learn to manage over time, you're negotiating with multiple clients who all think they're your only priority.

### Problem: Scope Creep

**In employment**: "Just one more feature" from your PM.

**With clients**: "Just one more feature" from Client A, Client B, and Client C—all in the same week. And they're all paying you, so saying no feels risky.

**The difference**: The scope creep is multiplied by the number of clients. And each client's "small ask" compounds into chaos.

### Problem: Meetings That Should Be Emails

**In employment**: 30-minute standup that should be a Slack update.

**With clients**: Each client wants their own check-in call. You spend 2 hours a day in "quick syncs" instead of building.

**The difference**: You can't skip these meetings—they're paying you. And each client deserves attention. So now you're in meetings all day across multiple projects.

### Problem: Lack of Context

**In employment**: You sometimes don't understand why decisions are made.

**With clients**: You definitely don't understand their business context, their internal politics, or why they're asking for what they're asking for.

**The difference**: At least in employment you can build context over time. With clients, especially short engagements, you're always working with partial information.

### Problem: Not Feeling Valued

**In employment**: Sometimes you feel like a cog in a machine.

**With clients**: You're definitely a vendor. You're brought in for specific tasks. You're external. And you're the first to go when budgets get cut.

**The difference**: In employment you at least have the possibility of becoming integral to the team. With clients, you're always somewhat disposable.

## The Problems That Actually Are Better

To be fair, some things do improve:

### You Can Fire Bad Clients (Eventually)

In employment, if you have a bad boss, your options are:
- Deal with it
- Transfer teams (if possible)
- Quit (and lose stability)

With clients, you can:
- Finish the current engagement and not renew
- Raise your rates so they leave
- Actually fire them if it's really bad

**The catch**: You need enough other clients or runway to afford to fire anyone. When you're desperate for money, you'll work with people you hate.

### You Can Raise Your Rates

In employment, salary increases are:
- Annual at best
- Usually 3-5%
- Subject to company policy
- Require negotiation

With clients, you can:
- Raise rates between projects
- Charge different clients different amounts
- Test higher rates with new clients
- Scale income faster

**The catch**: You have to actually be good at sales and able to justify your rates. And you have to be okay with some clients saying no.

### You Can Say No (Sometimes)

In employment, saying no to projects means:
- Not being a "team player"
- Potentially affecting your career
- Limited political capital to spend

With clients, you can:
- Say no to projects that don't fit
- Refer them to others
- Only take work that excites you

**The catch**: Only when you have enough work. When you're desperate, you say yes to everything.

### You Can Work From Anywhere

In employment, remote work is:
- Subject to company policy
- Might require being in specific time zones
- Sometimes requires office days

With clients, you can:
- Work from anywhere
- Set your own schedule (mostly)
- Structure your day how you want

**The catch**: Client meetings still happen. And if you have clients across time zones, you might be taking calls at 6 AM and 9 PM to accommodate everyone.

## The Hidden Problems Nobody Warns You About

Beyond the obvious stuff, here are the problems I didn't see coming:

### Decision Fatigue Multiplied

In employment:
- One set of priorities
- One codebase to learn
- One set of tools and processes
- One company culture to navigate

With multiple clients:
- Different priorities for each
- Different codebases, different tech stacks
- Different tools, different processes for everything
- Different communication styles, expectations, definitions of "done"

**The result**: By Wednesday you've made more decisions than you used to make in a month. You're exhausted, and you haven't even built anything yet.

### Context Switching Is Brutal

Monday morning:
- 9 AM: Client A (React, e-commerce)
- 11 AM: Client B (Python, data pipeline)
- 2 PM: Client C (Vue, internal tools)
- 4 PM: Back to Client A

Each switch requires:
- Loading different codebases into your head
- Remembering where you left off
- Switching communication styles
- Changing what "good" looks like for this client

**The result**: You spend more time switching contexts than actually being productive in any single context.

### You're Always "On"

In employment:
- Work hours are defined
- You can mostly disconnect outside them
- Vacation is actually vacation
- Sick days are a thing

With clients:
- Each client expects availability
- You can't ignore Client B's urgent message just because it's your weekend
- Vacation means hoping nothing breaks
- Sick days mean falling behind with everyone

**The result**: The boundaries you wanted from leaving employment completely disappear. You're more "on" than you ever were.

### The Gap Anxiety

In employment:
- Job is secure (mostly)
- Paycheck is predictable
- You can plan months or years ahead

With clients:
- Every project ends
- What happens after this?
- Constantly thinking about the next client
- Can't fully relax into the current work

**The result**: Even when you have work, you're worried about not having work. The anxiety never fully goes away.

### Client Quality Varies Wildly

In employment:
- You get to know your manager
- You learn what they value
- You can build a relationship over time
- Predictability increases

With clients:
- Every client is different
- Some are amazing, some are nightmares
- You don't know until you're already working together
- By the time you learn, you're locked in for the engagement

**The result**: 20% of your clients create 80% of your stress. But they're also 20% of your income.

## The Types of Clients You'll Encounter

After years of client work, I can categorize most clients into a few types:

### The Dream Client (Rare)

- Clear about what they want
- Respects your expertise
- Pays on time
- Reasonable about deadlines
- Good at communication
- Gives you creative freedom within constraints

**What to do**: Do great work, overdeliver slightly, maintain the relationship forever.

### The Indecisive Client (Common)

- "I'll know it when I see it"
- Changes requirements constantly
- Can't make decisions
- Wants you to read their mind
- Frustrated when you can't

**What to do**: Over-communicate, document everything, charge for revisions, set very clear boundaries.

### The Micromanager (Exhausting)

- Wants daily updates
- Questions every decision
- Doesn't trust your expertise
- Hired you but won't let you do the job

**What to do**: Provide detailed documentation, set expectations about communication cadence, consider not renewing.

### The Disappearing Client (Frustrating)

- Hires you urgently
- Then vanishes
- Doesn't respond to questions
- Reappears at the deadline angry you didn't finish
- Blames you for delays caused by their absence

**What to do**: Document all communication, set clear expectations about response times, charge for delays caused by their unavailability.

### The Budget Client (Often Not Worth It)

- Wants cheap work
- Questions every hour
- Asks for discounts constantly
- Doesn't value your time

**What to do**: Either charge properly and accept they'll leave, or decline upfront. These clients rarely become less difficult.

### The Unicorn Client (Treasure These)

- Becomes a long-term relationship
- Trusts you completely
- Pays well and on time
- Refers other clients
- Appreciates your work

**What to do**: Prioritize them, give them first access to your time, maintain the relationship even during quiet periods.

## What Makes Client Work Harder Than Employment

<Info>
**Why is managing clients harder than managing a boss?**
A boss is one relationship you can invest in learning over time. Clients are multiple relationships, each different, constantly churning. You're always adapting to new people, new expectations, new communication styles—while also doing the actual work.
</Info>

### Relationship Management at Scale

Employment: Learn to manage one boss, one team, one set of politics.

Clients: Learn to manage 3-5 different relationships simultaneously, each with different expectations, communication styles, and needs.

### Always Selling

Employment: You sell yourself once (interview), then focus on delivery.

Clients: You're always selling. Selling to new clients. Selling scope changes. Selling timeline extensions. Selling rate increases. Selling renewals.

### All the Business Stuff

Employment: Sales, marketing, accounting, legal, IT—someone else handles these.

Clients: You handle all of it. Every hour spent on business operations is an hour not billed.

### Emotional Labor

Employment: You can have bad days. Your team understands.

Clients: You can't show weakness. You're always "on." Even when you're struggling, you have to appear confident and capable.

### No Safety Net

Employment: Bad week? Still get paid. Sick? Paid time off. Slow quarter? Not your problem.

Clients: Bad week? You don't bill. Sick? You fall behind. Client cancels? Income disappears immediately.

## When Client Work Actually Makes Sense

Despite all this, client work can be the right choice if:

### You Have Multiple Clients

Never depend on one client for >50% of income. Ideally no client is >30%. The diversification reduces the stress of any single relationship.

### You Charge Enough

If you're charging 2-3x what you'd make employed (hourly), the tradeoffs can be worth it. If you're charging the same or less, you're getting a worse deal on every dimension.

### You Can Say No

Only when you have enough work to turn down bad fits. If you're taking every inquiry, you don't actually have freedom.

### You Enjoy Variety

If context switching energizes you instead of draining you, client work can be interesting. For me, it's exhausting.

### You're Good at Sales

If you enjoy selling and client relationships, this can be energizing. If you hate it, you'll be miserable.

### It's a Bridge, Not a Destination

Client work can be great as a step between employment and products. It teaches you business skills and generates cash flow while you build your own thing.

But as a permanent model? For most people, it's exhausting.

## My Experience: The Reality Check

I've done client work in various forms for years. Here's what I've learned about myself:

**What I liked:**
- Higher income than employment
- Variety of problems
- Some flexibility
- Could work remotely

**What I hated:**
- Constant context switching drained me
- Relationships with clients felt transactional
- Always selling, always performing
- Gap anxiety between projects was exhausting
- Missed colleagues and domain depth

**What I'm doing now:**
Moving toward a portfolio approach:
- Some employment (stability, colleagues, depth)
- Some products (ownership, upside)
- Maybe some consulting (variety, high rates)

But pure client work as my main income? I'm done with that.

## Questions to Ask Before You Make the Jump

Thinking about leaving employment for client work? Ask yourself:

### About Selling
- Do I enjoy selling or dread it?
- Can I handle rejection repeatedly?
- Do I have a network that could become clients?
- Am I comfortable promoting myself?

### About Money
- Do I have 6-12 months of runway?
- Can I handle irregular income?
- What's my actual burn rate?
- How much do I need to charge to make this work?

### About Isolation
- Do I work well alone or need colleagues?
- Where will I get professional feedback?
- How will I handle the isolation?
- Do I have other social outlets?

### About Work Style
- Does context switching energize or drain me?
- Do I prefer depth or breadth?
- Can I handle multiple relationships simultaneously?
- Am I good at setting boundaries?

### About Reality
- What am I actually running from?
- Will those problems follow me to client work?
- What do I think will be better?
- Am I being realistic about the tradeoffs?

## The Path I Didn't Expect

<Info>
**Is going back to employment after freelancing "failing"?**
No. It's recognizing what you actually need at this stage of life. I'm 37, not 27. Stability, colleagues, and going deep matter more to me now than maximum freedom. That's not failure—that's self-awareness.
</Info>

At 37, after years of client work, I'm interviewing for employment again.

Some people might see this as going backwards. I see it as learning what I actually value:

- Colleagues over isolation
- Depth over breadth
- Stability over maximum income
- Clear boundaries over "freedom"

That doesn't mean client work was a mistake. It taught me:
- How to sell
- How to manage relationships
- What I actually value in work
- What kind of problems I want to solve

Now I can make an informed choice instead of running from something.

## The Truth About Freedom

Here's what nobody tells you:

**Trading one boss for many isn't freedom.**
**It's just more bosses.**

Real freedom might actually be:
- Choosing employment with good boundaries
- Building products on the side
- Having financial stability so you can say no
- Working with people you respect

Freedom isn't about who signs your paycheck. It's about having options and making intentional choices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="How many clients is too many?">
    For me, more than 3 active clients meant constant context switching and exhaustion. For some people, 5+ is fine. It depends on: project complexity, client communication needs, your capacity for context switching, and how much administrative overhead each requires. If you're stressed and not shipping, you have too many.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you find good clients?">
    Honestly: referrals from existing good clients. Otherwise: build in public, share your work, contribute to communities, be helpful without expecting immediate return. The best clients find you through reputation, not cold outreach. But this takes time to build.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you fire a bad client?">
    Options: (1) Finish current engagement, don't renew. (2) Raise rates high enough they leave. (3) Actually fire them if it's toxic—professional but direct: "This isn't a good fit, let's transition to someone better suited." Have runway before you do this.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What should you charge as a freelancer or consultant?">
    Minimum: 2x your employed hourly rate (to cover gaps, taxes, benefits, business overhead). Better: 3x. Premium: 4x+. Don't compete on price—compete on value, specialization, and results. If you're the cheapest option, you'll attract the worst clients.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you handle gap anxiety between projects?">
    Some options: (1) Always be selling, even when busy. (2) Build recurring revenue (retainers). (3) Have 6-12 months runway. (4) Diversify income sources. (5) Accept it as part of the model and plan accordingly. Honestly, it never fully goes away—you just get better at managing it.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can you have boundaries with clients?">
    Yes, but you have to set them from the start and enforce them. Define: working hours, response times, communication channels, scope of work, revision limits. The best clients respect boundaries. Bad clients push them constantly—that's your signal to not renew.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you handle clients who don't pay?">
    Prevention: (1) Contracts. (2) Deposits upfront. (3) Milestone-based payment. (4) Stop work if payment is late. If they still don't pay: small claims court for smaller amounts, collections for larger. Learn from it and tighten your process.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Is it normal to hate client work after a while?">
    Yes. The things that seem exciting at first (variety, freedom, multiple projects) often become exhausting over time (context switching, lack of depth, constant selling). Many people use client work as a bridge to something else—employment, products, or a hybrid model.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you know if you should go back to employment?">
    If you: miss colleagues, want to go deep instead of staying broad, are tired of the sales process, value stability more than maximum income, want clear boundaries, or just prefer the employment model at this life stage. It's not failure—it's choosing what fits your actual needs.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What's the best thing about client work?">
    For me: learning to sell, manage relationships, and run a business. Also: the confidence that comes from knowing you can generate income independently. Even if you go back to employment, those skills are valuable. You know you have options.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
