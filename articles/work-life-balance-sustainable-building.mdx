## The Trap of Constant Productivity

When you're building something on the side—whether that's a product, a service, a business—there's this constant pressure that every free moment should be productive.

Weekend? Build.
Evening? Build.
Holiday? Build.

After all, that's what the success stories say, right? "I spent every weekend for two years building this before it took off."

What they don't tell you:
- How many of those people burned out before they got anywhere
- How many relationships suffered
- How many actually sustainable businesses could have been built with a more balanced approach
- How many quit because the grind became unsustainable

## My Journey Through Different Work Models

Let me share where I've been over the past 15 years:

1. **Full-time roles** - One boss, clear boundaries, domain expertise
2. **Freelance PPC and analytics** - Multiple clients, more freedom, less stability
3. **Consulting** - Higher rates, project-based work
4. **Internal tools** - Building for specific companies
5. **Growth engineering** - Working at the intersection of code and revenue
6. **Running a voucher publisher for 5 years** - Full entrepreneurship, all the risk
7. **Back to client work** - Short MVP sprints, 6-8 weeks per project
8. **Now interviewing for senior engineering roles in Prague** - While still building on the side

It hasn't been a straight line. I've traded one boss for many. Learned a lot. Lost money. Made money. Lost more.

And here's what I've learned: **each model has different demands on your time, energy, and mental health.**

## The Reality of "Freedom"

<Info>
**What's the difference between one boss and many?**
In a job you have one boss. With clients you have many. The problems you hate in full-time work often show up in client work too—some clients are great, some are not, and you're still a service at the end of the day.
</Info>

When I was working full-time, I thought: "If I could just work for myself, I'd have freedom."

Then I started doing client work and realized:

**In a job you have one boss.**
**With clients you have many.**

Some clients are great. Some are... not. And you're still providing a service at the end of the day.

The problems I wanted to escape from full-time work? Many of them followed me:
- Unreasonable deadlines
- Scope creep
- Meetings that should have been emails
- People who don't respect your time
- Having to do work you're not excited about because you need the money

The difference is now I have to manage all of this across multiple clients instead of one employer.

## What I Didn't Appreciate About Employment

At 37, I'm realizing something I couldn't see at 25:

**Employment provides things you don't appreciate until they're gone:**

1. **Stability** - Knowing exactly when money hits your account
2. **Domain expertise** - Going deep in one area instead of shallow in many
3. **Colleagues** - People to discuss problems with who actually understand the context
4. **Clear boundaries** - When you clock out, you're actually out
5. **Benefits** - Health insurance, paid leave, equipment budgets
6. **Learning** - Working with people better than you in specific areas

I traded all of that for freedom. And sometimes freedom feels like chaos.

## Why I'm Shifting Again

That's why I'm making another change. Moving from short 6-8 week MVP sprints toward longer-term engagements. Minimum 6 months.

Why?
- Different rhythm
- Different responsibilities
- More depth instead of breadth
- Less context switching
- More sustainable mentally

And I'm interviewing for senior engineering roles while still building products on the side.

Some people might see this as "going backwards." I see it as **building a portfolio approach** that actually fits my life at 37.

## Lessons from Net-90 and Worse

Here's something they don't tell you about certain business models:

I spent 5 years running a voucher publisher. Built it up. Good revenue. Then overnight, affiliate policies changed and revenue vanished.

Net-90 payment terms? Try net-180. Try "we changed our policy so we're not paying that revenue at all."

If someone had explained that upfront, I might have chosen a different business to scale entirely.

But the upside? **Resilience. Adaptability. Comfort with risk and chaos.**

Those are valuable skills. Expensive to learn, but valuable.

## The Portfolio Approach

<Info>
**What is a portfolio career?**
Instead of putting all your eggs in one basket (employment OR freelancing OR products), you deliberately mix different types of work to balance stability with freedom, depth with breadth, and guaranteed income with upside potential.
</Info>

I'm leaning toward a portfolio approach:
- Some employment (stability, colleagues, depth)
- Some consulting (higher rates, project variety)
- Some products (upside, ownership, creativity)

This isn't a compromise. It's intentional design.

**Employment** gives me:
- Stable base income
- Colleagues and domain expertise
- Clear boundaries
- Benefits

**Consulting** gives me:
- Higher hourly rates
- Variety of problems
- Control over who I work with
- Flexibility

**Products** give me:
- Ownership of what I build
- Uncapped upside
- Creative freedom
- Something that compounds

Together? They create a system that's more resilient than any single approach.

## Why Breaks Make You Stronger

Back to where we started: taking a real break this weekend.

Here's what I've learned about breaks:

**Breaks are not**:
- Weakness
- Wasted time
- Something you earn after you've "made it"
- Optional

**Breaks are**:
- How you avoid burnout
- When your brain processes complex problems
- What lets you see clearly enough to make good decisions
- Non-negotiable for sustainable building

The most productive periods of my career have come after deliberate breaks, not during grinds.

When you step away:
- Problems that seemed impossible become obvious
- Ideas you've been forcing start to flow naturally
- Priorities become clear
- Energy comes back

**You can't build something sustainable if you're not sustainable yourself.**

## Practical Framework: Assessing Your Current Situation

Use these questions to honestly assess where you are:

### Energy
- Am I energized or drained by my current work?
- When was the last time I took a real break?
- Am I building from abundance or desperation?

### Financial
- How many months of runway do I have?
- Am I making decisions based on what I need or what I want?
- What's my actual burn rate vs. what I think it is?

### Emotional
- Who can I talk to about the real challenges?
- Am I carrying emotional load that should be distributed?
- What support systems do I have beyond my partner?

### Strategic
- Am I moving with intention or reacting?
- What am I optimizing for right now?
- Is my current path actually taking me where I want to go?

### Practical
- What's working that I should do more of?
- What's definitely not working that I should stop?
- What's one small change that could make things more sustainable?

## What Sustainable Actually Means

<Info>
**How do you know if your approach is sustainable?**
If you can maintain it for 5 years without burning out, damaging your health, ruining your relationships, or needing to make a dramatic change—that's sustainable. If not, something needs to adjust.
</Info>

Ask yourself: **Can I do this for 5 years?**

Not "can I grind through it for 5 years" but "can I maintain this pace, this approach, this lifestyle for 5 years without:"
- Burning out
- Damaging my health
- Ruining my relationships
- Needing to make a dramatic change

If the answer is no, something needs to adjust now, not later.

## The Three-Day Weekend Test

Here's a simple test of whether your current approach is sustainable:

**Can you take a three-day weekend without:**
- Feeling guilty the entire time
- Checking Slack/email constantly
- Thinking about work nonstop
- Coming back more exhausted than when you left

If you can't, you're not building sustainably. You're grinding. And grinding has an expiration date.

## Starting From Where You Are

<Info>
**What should you do next?**
Don't try to overhaul everything. Pick one thing that would make your current situation more sustainable and do that. Maybe it's taking real weekends. Maybe it's saying no to one type of client. Maybe it's scheduling actual breaks. Start small, build from there.
</Info>

You don't need to quit your job or fire all your clients or stop building products.

You just need to ask: **What would make this more sustainable?**

Maybe it's:
- Actually taking weekends off
- Saying no to one type of client
- Building in one-week breaks between projects
- Cutting scope on your side project
- Finding one person who gets it to talk to
- Admitting that your current pace isn't working

Pick one thing. Do that. Build from there.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="How do you know when you need a break vs. when you just need to push through?">
    If you're asking the question, you probably need a break. Real productivity requires rest. The times I've "pushed through" usually resulted in work I had to redo later anyway. Breaks aren't earned rewards—they're necessary maintenance. If you can't think clearly, you can't make good decisions.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What's the difference between freelancing and client work?">
    In practice, they're similar—you're trading time/expertise for money. The main difference is typically duration and structure. Freelancing often means shorter, task-based work. Client work (as I use it) usually means longer engagements, 6-8 week sprints or more. Both have the same core challenge: you have multiple bosses instead of one.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Why would you go back to employment after experiencing freedom?">
    Because "freedom" without stability often just means chaos with extra steps. Employment provides: stable income, colleagues, domain expertise, clear boundaries, and benefits. At 37, those things matter differently than they did at 25. It's not going backwards—it's choosing the right mix for this stage of life.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you handle multiple clients without burning out?">
    Honestly? I haven't figured it out perfectly. But what helps: clear boundaries, saying no to bad-fit clients, charging enough that you can be selective, building in breaks between projects, and having systems for everything repeatable. The portfolio approach helps too—mixing client work with other income sources reduces desperation.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What's a portfolio career and how do you build one?">
    A portfolio career means deliberately mixing different types of work—employment, consulting, products—to balance stability with freedom. You build it gradually: start with your base (usually employment or main client work), add one other income stream, test it, refine it, maybe add another. The goal is resilience, not juggling everything at once.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you know if you're building sustainably?">
    Ask: can I maintain this for 5 years without burning out, damaging my health, ruining relationships, or needing a dramatic change? If no, it's not sustainable. Also: are you taking real breaks? Can you step away for a weekend without guilt? Are you making decisions from abundance or desperation?
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What if I can't afford to take breaks?">
    If you genuinely can't afford a weekend off, that's a sign your situation isn't sustainable. But often "can't afford" means "feel guilty about." Start small: take Sunday fully off. Then work up to a full weekend. Then a three-day weekend. Breaks make you more productive, not less. You're not losing time—you're investing in better decisions.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you deal with the isolation of working alone?">
    This is one I'm still figuring out. What helps: coworking spaces, online communities (like this one), scheduling regular calls with other builders, taking on some client work just for the human interaction. Employment can also provide this—another reason the portfolio approach appeals to me.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What's the biggest mistake you've made in 15 years?">
    Scaling a business model (voucher publisher) without understanding how fragile the revenue model was. Net-90 and net-180 payment terms, then policy changes that wiped out revenue overnight. If I'd understood the risks better, I would have chosen a different business to scale. Expensive lesson in resilience, though.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What would you tell your 25-year-old self?">
    Employment isn't prison—it's stability you'll miss later. Colleagues are valuable. Going deep beats going wide. The problems you're running from will follow you to client work. Breaks make you stronger. And the most sustainable path isn't always the most "free" one.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
