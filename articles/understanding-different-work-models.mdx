## The Grass Is Always Greener

<Info>
**Why do we always want what we don't have?**
When you're employed, you dream of freedom. When you're freelancing, you miss stability. The truth: every work model has tradeoffs, and understanding them before you jump saves years of frustration.
</Info>

When I was working full-time, I thought: "If I could just work for myself, everything would be better."

When I went freelance, I thought: "If I had more stable clients, this would work."

When I had stable clients, I thought: "If I could build products, I'd have real freedom."

When I was building products, I thought: "Some employment stability would be nice right now."

After 15 years of trying every model, here's what I've learned: **they all have tradeoffs, and the "best" model depends entirely on your context.**

## The Four Main Work Models

Let's break down the four main ways to make money as a builder:

1. **Full-time employment**
2. **Freelancing**
3. **Client work** (longer engagements)
4. **Products** (your own or someone else's)

Each has a different impact on:
- **Your time** - How much control you have
- **Your money** - Stability vs. upside
- **Your mental health** - Stress, isolation, boundaries
- **Your learning** - Depth vs. breadth
- **Your optionality** - Future opportunities

Let's examine each honestly.

## Full-Time Employment

### What It Actually Is

You work for one company. Clear hours (usually). Regular paycheck. Benefits. One boss. Clear role.

### The Upsides (That You Don't Appreciate Until They're Gone)

**Stability**
- Money hits your account like clockwork
- No worrying about where next month's rent comes from
- Can plan long-term (mortgage, vacation, investments)

**Domain Expertise**
- You go deep in one area
- Become genuinely excellent at something specific
- Build expertise that compounds

**Colleagues**
- People who understand the context
- Can discuss complex problems without 30 minutes of background
- Social interaction built into your day
- Learning from people better than you

**Clear Boundaries**
- When you clock out, you're out
- Weekends are actually weekends
- Vacation is actually vacation
- Someone else worries about the business

**Benefits**
- Health insurance
- Paid time off
- Equipment budgets
- Professional development
- 401k/pension
- Sometimes: gym, food, commuter benefits

**Lower Stress**
- One set of priorities
- Clear success metrics
- Predictable rhythm
- Not worrying about sales/marketing/admin

### The Downsides (Why People Want to Leave)

**Limited Freedom**
- Can't choose your schedule
- Can't choose your projects (usually)
- Can't choose your team
- Have to be available during specific hours

**Income Ceiling**
- Salary increases are capped
- No direct upside from company growth (usually)
- Trading time for money at a fixed rate

**Office Politics**
- Bad managers exist
- Reorganizations happen
- You might get assigned to boring projects
- Promotion often depends on politics, not just merit

**Lack of Ownership**
- You don't own what you build
- Can't take it with you
- All the upside goes to the company

**Risk**
- Can be laid off
- Tied to one company's success
- All eggs in one basket

### Best For

- People who value stability over maximum freedom
- Those wanting to go deep in one domain
- Anyone who needs benefits (health insurance, visa sponsorship)
- People early in their career building expertise
- Those who prefer clear boundaries between work and life
- Anyone who gets energy from working with a team

### Red Flags

- If you hate your industry, employment won't fix that
- If you have a bad manager, the benefits might not be worth it
- If the company culture is toxic, stability isn't enough
- If you're staying only for the paycheck, you'll be miserable

## Freelancing

### What It Actually Is

Project-based work, usually shorter engagements (days to weeks). Multiple clients. You handle everything: sales, delivery, admin, taxes.

### The Upsides

**Freedom**
- Choose your schedule
- Choose your clients
- Work from anywhere
- Take time off when you want (if you can afford it)

**Variety**
- Different problems
- Different industries
- Different technologies
- Never boring

**Higher Hourly Rates**
- Usually charge 2-3x what you'd make as an employee hourly
- Can raise rates as you gain experience
- Market-rate pricing

**Control**
- Fire bad clients
- Say no to projects that don't excite you
- Build your business your way

### The Downsides

**Unstable Income**
- Feast or famine
- Hard to plan long-term
- Constantly selling
- Net-30, net-60, sometimes net-never payment terms

**You're a Vendor**
- Clients can drop you anytime
- First to go in budget cuts
- Limited context and influence
- Often don't see projects through

**Everything Is Your Job**
- Sales
- Marketing
- Delivery
- Admin
- Accounting
- Tech support
- Collections

**No Benefits**
- Pay for your own health insurance
- No paid time off
- No retirement contributions
- Equipment costs come out of pocket

**Isolation**
- Working alone
- No colleagues to discuss problems with
- Easy to feel disconnected
- Hard to get feedback

**Constant Context Switching**
- Different clients
- Different codebases
- Different domains
- Mental overhead is exhausting

### Best For

- People who value variety over depth
- Those who can handle income instability
- Anyone good at sales and self-promotion
- People who work well alone
- Those with a strong network for leads
- Anyone who needs maximum schedule flexibility

### Red Flags

- If you hate selling, you'll struggle to find clients
- If you need stability, the income swings will destroy you mentally
- If you're bad with money, irregular income is dangerous
- If you need people, isolation will drain you

## Client Work (Longer Engagements)

### What It Actually Is

Similar to freelancing but longer: 6 weeks to 6 months or more. Often retainer-based. Deeper relationships. More like having 2-3 "bosses" instead of 10.

### The Upsides

**More Stable Than Freelancing**
- Predictable income for the engagement period
- Can plan months ahead instead of weeks
- Less time spent on sales

**Deeper Work**
- Actually finish projects
- See impact of your work
- Build meaningful relationships
- Go deeper into problem domains

**Better Rates**
- Often monthly retainers
- Charge for value, not just hours
- Can raise rates between engagements

**Some Flexibility**
- Usually remote
- More control than employment
- Can still take other clients if structured right

### The Downsides

**Multiple Bosses**
- Each client has different expectations
- Different communication styles
- Different standards of quality
- Different levels of respect for your time

**Still a Service**
- You're delivering what they want
- Limited creative control
- Projects end
- You don't own the upside

**Scope Creep**
- "Just one more thing"
- Boundaries blur over time
- Hard to say no when they're paying monthly

**Between-Project Anxiety**
- What happens when this ends?
- Constantly thinking about next client
- Can't fully relax into the work

**Client Management**
- Unreasonable deadlines still exist
- Some clients are great, some aren't
- You can't always choose (especially starting out)
- The problems you hated in employment follow you here

### Best For

- People who want more stability than freelancing but more freedom than employment
- Those who can handle 2-5 client relationships at once
- Anyone who likes finishing projects but doesn't need to own them
- People good at managing expectations and boundaries
- Those who want higher income potential than employment

### Red Flags

- If you struggle with boundaries, clients will consume your life
- If you hate client management, having 3 clients means 3x the headache
- If you need stability, the gaps between engagements will stress you out
- If you want ownership, you'll resent building for others

## Products (Your Own)

### What It Actually Is

You build something you own. You control the roadmap. All the upside is yours. So is all the risk.

### The Upsides

**Ownership**
- It's yours
- You build it how you want
- All upside belongs to you
- Can sell it later if it works

**Creative Freedom**
- No one telling you what to build
- Can experiment
- Can say no to features that don't make sense
- Build for the long term, not the next sprint

**Uncapped Upside**
- If it works, the revenue scales
- Not trading time for money directly
- Passive income potential (eventually)
- Wealth creation potential

**Learning**
- Every part of the business
- Marketing, sales, support, development
- Breadth of experience

**Satisfaction**
- It's yours
- You can be proud of it
- Users choose you, not because their company bought you

### The Downsides

**All the Risk**
- Might make $0
- Might make $0 for years
- Your time, your money, your risk
- Failure is expensive

**You Do Everything**
- Development
- Marketing
- Sales
- Support
- Accounting
- Legal
- Everything

**Loneliness**
- Building alone is isolating
- Hard to find people who get it
- Every decision is yours
- Easy to spiral into self-doubt

**No Safety Net**
- No paycheck
- No benefits
- No team to fall back on
- All problems are your problems

**Market Risk**
- You might build something no one wants
- Market might change
- Competitors might crush you
- Platform risk (if you build on someone else's platform)

**Slow**
- Takes time to get traction
- Takes time to build
- Takes time to learn what works
- Can feel like you're getting nowhere for months/years

### Best For

- People with runway (savings or another income source)
- Those who can handle uncertainty and risk
- Anyone who has to own what they build
- People who can do sales, marketing, and code
- Those who are okay being alone a lot
- Anyone who believes they can build something valuable

### Red Flags

- If you need income soon, products take too long
- If you hate marketing, you'll build something no one knows about
- If you need validation from others, the silence will destroy you
- If you can't finish projects, you'll just collect side projects

## The Reality: Most People Need a Mix

<Info>
**What's the optimal work model?**
There isn't one. The "best" model changes based on your age, financial situation, family, goals, and risk tolerance. Most sustainable careers mix multiple models to balance stability with upside.
</Info>

Here's what I've learned: **pure models are rare and often unsustainable.**

Most people who seem to "have it figured out" are actually mixing:

- Employment + side products
- Freelancing + one product
- 2-3 clients + teaching
- Full-time + consulting
- Products + consulting

The portfolio approach lets you:
- **Balance risk** - If one fails, you're not broke
- **Balance learning** - Depth from one, breadth from others
- **Balance income** - Stable base + upside potential
- **Balance social needs** - People from one, independence from another

## How to Choose What's Right for You

Ask yourself these questions:

### Financial Situation
- How much runway do I have?
- Can I handle irregular income?
- Do I need benefits (health insurance, visa)?
- What's my actual burn rate?

### Risk Tolerance
- How much uncertainty can I handle?
- Do I need to know where money comes from next month?
- Can I sleep at night without a stable paycheck?

### Social Needs
- Do I need colleagues or work well alone?
- Where do I get energy: from people or from solitude?
- Do I have other social outlets if work doesn't provide them?

### Learning Style
- Do I prefer going deep or staying broad?
- Do I get bored easily or love mastery?
- What kind of expertise am I trying to build?

### Control Needs
- How important is owning what I build?
- Can I be happy building for others?
- Do I need creative freedom or clear direction?

### Life Stage
- What are my actual constraints?
- Family? Mortgage? Visa? Health?
- What can I actually afford to risk?

### Time Horizon
- Am I optimizing for next year or next decade?
- Can I delay gratification or need wins now?
- What's my actual timeline?

## My Path Through All of Them

Here's how I've moved through these over 15 years:

**Early 20s: Employment**
- Needed: Stability, learning, visa sponsorship
- Got: Domain expertise, colleagues, stable income
- Missed: Freedom, upside

**Late 20s: Freelancing**
- Needed: Freedom, variety, higher income
- Got: Flexibility, breadth of experience, good money during booms
- Missed: Stability, depth, colleagues

**Early 30s: Products (Voucher Publisher)**
- Needed: Ownership, upside potential
- Got: Business education, resilience, some success
- Missed: When policies changed, the whole thing collapsed

**Mid 30s: Client Work (MVP Sprints)**
- Needed: More stable than freelancing, more freedom than employment
- Got: Good money, interesting projects, some flexibility
- Missed: The gaps between projects were stressful

**Now (37): Portfolio Approach**
- Trying: Employment + consulting + products
- Hoping for: Stability from employment, upside from products, variety from consulting
- We'll see: If this works long-term

## The Model Doesn't Matter If You Hate the Work

<Info>
**What matters more than the work model?**
The actual work itself. A toxic full-time job is worse than chaotic freelancing. Freelancing for clients you hate is worse than employment you enjoy. The model is just a container—what you put in it matters more.
</Info>

Here's the truth that took me too long to learn:

**The work model doesn't matter if you hate the work itself.**

- A toxic full-time job is worse than chaotic freelancing
- Freelancing for clients you hate is worse than employment with a good team
- Building a product no one wants is worse than consulting for clients who value you

Get clear on:
1. What work actually energizes you
2. Who you want to work with
3. What problems you want to solve

Then choose the model that best supports that, given your constraints.

## Switching Is Normal

One more thing: switching between models isn't failure.

I've gone:
- Employment → Freelancing → Products → Client Work → Back to Employment (probably)

Each shift made sense at the time. Each taught me something I couldn't have learned otherwise.

The people who tell you to "pick a lane" usually haven't tried multiple lanes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Which work model makes the most money?">
    It depends. Employment has the lowest ceiling but highest floor. Products have the highest ceiling but lowest floor (can be $0). Freelancing and client work are in between. For most people, a mix of models generates the most over a career—stable base + upside potential.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can you do multiple models at once?">
    Yes, and I'd argue you should. Employment + side product. Freelancing + teaching. 2-3 clients + building in public. The portfolio approach balances risk and gives you optionality. Just don't try to do everything at once—start with one base, add others gradually.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you know when it's time to switch models?">
    When your current model stops fitting your life. Maybe you need more stability (go toward employment). Maybe you need more upside (go toward products). Maybe you're burned out on client work (try employment for a while). Listen to what your actual life needs, not what you think you "should" want.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What if I choose the wrong model?">
    You probably will, and that's fine. I've "chosen wrong" multiple times. Each time taught me something. Models aren't permanent commitments—they're experiments. Try one, learn from it, adjust. The only real mistake is staying in something that's clearly not working because you're afraid to change.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How long should you try a model before switching?">
    Depends on the model. Employment: at least a year unless it's toxic. Freelancing: 6 months to build momentum. Products: long enough to ship and get real feedback (months, not years). But if something is clearly wrong (toxic culture, unsustainable pace, crushing your mental health), leave sooner.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Why would anyone choose employment over freelancing if freelancing pays more per hour?">
    Because hourly rate isn't everything. Employment includes: stability, benefits, colleagues, clear boundaries, paid time off, learning opportunities, and lower stress. For some people at some life stages, those matter more than maximum hourly rate. Plus, freelancing has gaps, so annual income might be similar.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can you build products while employed?">
    Yes, and it's often the best approach. Employment gives you: stable income (reduces desperation), time boundaries (forcing efficiency), learning (what works at scale), and colleagues (reducing isolation). Many successful products started as side projects while the founder was employed.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you handle the mental shift between models?">
    Honestly, it's hard. Going from employment to freelancing requires learning to sell yourself. Going from freelancing to products requires learning to work without external validation. Going back to employment requires accepting less control. Give yourself time to adjust—it takes months, not weeks.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What's the biggest mistake people make when choosing a work model?">
    Choosing based on what sounds good in theory instead of what fits their actual life. They hear "passive income" and quit their stable job without runway. They hear "freedom" and go freelance without considering if they can sell. They take employment when they really need ownership. Match the model to your reality, not your aspirations.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Is there a 'best' progression through work models?">
    Not really. Common paths: employment → freelancing → products, or employment → senior employment → consulting. But there's no "correct" order. I've gone employment → freelancing → products → client work → back to employment. Your path should be based on what you're learning and what your life needs, not a prescribed progression.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

---

_The "best" work model is the one that fits your actual life right now—not the one that sounds best or that someone else says you should want. Be honest about your constraints, your needs, and your goals. Then choose accordingly._
