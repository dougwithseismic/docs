## The False Choice

<Info>
**Do you have to choose between employment, freelancing, or products?**
No. The idea that you must pick one path is outdated. The most successful and resilient builders often mix multiple models: employment for stability, consulting for upside, products for ownership. It's not compromise—it's strategy.
</Info>

Most career advice presents a choice:

- **Employment** (stability but limited upside)
- **Freelancing** (freedom but unstable)
- **Products** (ownership but risky)

Pick one. Commit. That's your path.

But what if that's wrong? What if the most sustainable approach is doing multiple things simultaneously?

That's the **portfolio career approach**: deliberately mixing different work models to create something more resilient than any single path.

## What Is a Portfolio Career?

A portfolio career means:

**You intentionally combine multiple income sources and work models:**
- Employment + products
- Consulting + teaching
- Part-time role + full-time building
- 2-3 retainer clients + product development
- Employment + consulting + products

**Each serves a different purpose:**
- One for **stability** (base income, benefits)
- One for **upside** (higher rates, equity, ownership)
- One for **learning** (breadth or depth, depending on what you need)
- One for **optionality** (keeping skills fresh, building network)

**Together, they create resilience:**
- If one fails, you're not broke
- If one becomes unsustainable, you have others
- Each compensates for the weaknesses of the others
- You're not dependent on any single source

## Why the Portfolio Approach Works

### Resilience Through Diversification

Just like investing, putting all your eggs in one basket is risky:

**Single income source:**
- Employment: One layoff and you're unemployed
- Freelancing: Clients dry up and you're desperate
- Products: Market shifts and revenue disappears

**Portfolio approach:**
- If you lose your job, you have consulting and products
- If consulting slows down, you have employment base
- If product fails, you have stable income to try again
- No single failure destroys you

### Balancing Tradeoffs

Every work model has tradeoffs. The portfolio approach lets you get the best of each:

**Employment provides:**
- Stable income
- Benefits (healthcare, 401k)
- Colleagues and team
- Domain expertise
- Clear boundaries

**Consulting provides:**
- Higher rates (2-3x employment)
- Variety of problems
- Flexibility
- Control over who you work with

**Products provide:**
- Ownership
- Uncapped upside
- Creative freedom
- Long-term asset building

**Together, you get:**
- Stability AND upside
- Depth AND breadth
- People AND independence
- Salary AND equity potential

### Reduced Desperation

The biggest advantage: **you're never desperate.**

When employment is your only income:
- You stay in bad situations too long
- You accept low offers
- You have limited negotiating power

When freelancing is your only income:
- You take clients you hate
- You can't afford to say no
- Gap anxiety is constant

When products are your only income:
- You rush to monetize
- You make desperate decisions
- You can't wait for product-market fit

**With a portfolio:**
- You can walk away from bad situations
- You can say no to wrong-fit opportunities
- You can be patient with products
- You build from abundance, not desperation

## The Three Main Portfolio Models

### Model 1: Employment + Products

**Structure:**
- Full-time employed (40-50 hours/week)
- Build products evenings/weekends (10-20 hours/week)

**Employment provides:**
- Stable salary
- Benefits
- Colleagues
- Domain learning

**Products provide:**
- Ownership
- Upside potential
- Creative outlet
- Long-term asset

**Best for:**
- Early in career (need stable income)
- Risk-averse (want safety net)
- Building domain expertise
- Anyone who needs benefits

**Example:**
- Software engineer at a company
- Building SaaS product on weekends
- Employment pays bills, product builds wealth
- No rush to monetize, can build properly

**Challenges:**
- Limited time for product
- Energy management
- Potential IP conflicts (check contract)
- Slow progress can be frustrating

**My take:** This is probably the most sustainable model for most people.

### Model 2: Consulting + Products

**Structure:**
- Consulting 20-30 hours/week (3-4 days)
- Building products 10-20 hours/week (1-2 days)

**Consulting provides:**
- High hourly rate
- Flexibility
- Variety of problems
- Cash flow

**Products provide:**
- Ownership
- Upside potential
- Not trading time for money
- Long-term value

**Best for:**
- Mid-career (established reputation)
- Self-directed (good at finding clients)
- Prefer variety over depth
- Want more control than employment

**Example:**
- 3 days/week consulting at high rates
- 2 days/week building product
- Consulting funds product development
- Product potentially replaces consulting income

**Challenges:**
- Client work expands to fill time
- Context switching is exhausting
- Hard to say no to lucrative consulting
- Requires discipline to protect product time

**My take:** Works if you're good at sales and boundaries. Otherwise consulting consumes everything.

### Model 3: Employment + Consulting + Products (Full Portfolio)

**Structure:**
- Part-time or full-time employment (primary income)
- Occasional consulting (high-value only)
- Products (long-term building)

**Employment provides:**
- Base salary and benefits
- Colleagues and team
- Stability

**Consulting provides:**
- Extra income
- Variety
- Keep skills broad
- Higher rates

**Products provide:**
- Ownership
- Upside potential
- Future optionality

**Best for:**
- Established professionals (late career)
- Strong network (consulting comes to you)
- High energy (can manage multiple things)
- Want maximum optionality

**Example:**
- Senior engineer role 4 days/week
- High-end consulting 1 day/month
- Building product 10 hours/week
- Everything funds everything else

**Challenges:**
- Complexity (managing 3 different things)
- Time management is critical
- Risk of doing everything poorly
- Requires excellent boundaries

**My take:** This is where I'm headed at 37. We'll see if it works.

## How to Build a Portfolio Career

### Start with One Stable Base

Don't try to build multiple income streams simultaneously from zero.

**Start with:**
- Employment (most stable)
- OR 2-3 retainer clients (if you have them)
- OR existing product with revenue (rare)

**Then add:**
- One other income stream
- Test it
- Refine it
- Stabilize it
- Maybe add another

**Don't do:**
- Quit everything to build portfolio from scratch
- Try to juggle 5 things at once
- Add new streams before stabilizing current ones

### Protect Your Time Ruthlessly

**Each component gets dedicated time:**
- Employment: 40 hours/week
- Products: 10 hours/week (specific days/times)
- Consulting: 5 hours/week (strictly limited)

**Set boundaries:**
- Employment doesn't bleed into product time
- Consulting doesn't expand to fill all available time
- Products get consistent attention (even if just 5 hours/week)

**Use scheduling:**
- Block time for each component
- Treat product time like a meeting
- Say no to anything that conflicts

### Price Each Component Appropriately

**Employment:**
- Negotiate for 4-day weeks if possible
- Or remote/flexible schedule
- Value: stability and benefits

**Consulting:**
- Charge 3-4x employment rate
- Only take interesting work
- Value: high rates justify limited time

**Products:**
- Don't need immediate revenue (other income covers)
- Can be patient finding product-market fit
- Value: long-term upside

### Let Each Component Support the Others

**Employment supports products:**
- Stable income removes desperation
- Colleagues reduce isolation
- Domain expertise informs product ideas
- Benefits you don't have to think about

**Products support consulting:**
- Demo of your capabilities
- Marketing (building in public)
- Expertise that commands higher rates
- Eventually might replace consulting income

**Consulting supports products:**
- Cash flow for product development
- Real-world problems inform product direction
- Network for early users
- Keeps skills sharp

## Common Objections (and Responses)

### "Won't you do everything poorly?"

**Objection:** If you split time across multiple things, won't you be mediocre at all of them?

**Response:** Only if you try to do too much. That's why you:
- Start with one stable base
- Add components gradually
- Protect time ruthlessly
- Be selective about what you take on
- Let some things be 80% instead of 100%

### "Isn't this just lack of focus?"

**Objection:** Shouldn't you pick one thing and go all-in?

**Response:** Depends on what you're optimizing for:
- If you're 25 and building a startup, maybe yes
- If you're 35 with a family and mortgage, probably no
- If you want maximum optionality, portfolio approach gives you that
- If you want to bet everything on one thing, that's also valid

**Different goals, different approaches.**

### "Won't employers hate that you're building on the side?"

**Objection:** If you're employed, won't they want 100% of your focus?

**Response:** Some will, some won't. That's why you:
- Are transparent in interviews
- Don't work on competing products
- Respect IP agreements
- Do side work on your own time
- Deliver excellent work in your day job

**Many companies are fine with it. Work for those companies.**

### "Isn't this just being a consultant with extra steps?"

**Objection:** This sounds like consulting with a fancy name.

**Response:** No, because:
- Consulting alone is unstable
- Portfolio includes stable base (employment)
- Plus long-term asset building (products)
- It's fundamentally about diversification
- Not putting all eggs in one basket

### "Won't this take forever to build wealth?"

**Objection:** If you're splitting time, won't products grow slower?

**Response:** Maybe. But:
- Employment provides immediate income
- Consulting provides high rates
- Products can grow slowly without pressure
- And you're not going broke while building
- Slow and sustainable beats fast and unsustainable

## When Portfolio Approach Doesn't Work

**Don't do this if:**

### You're Building a Venture-Scale Company

If you're raising venture capital and trying to build a unicorn:
- You probably need 100% focus
- Investors expect full commitment
- Execution speed matters enormously
- Portfolio approach won't work

### You're in a Hyper-Competitive Market

If you're competing with well-funded companies:
- Your half-time effort won't beat their full-time team
- Speed to market is critical
- You need total focus to win

### You're Early in Career and Need Deep Expertise

If you're trying to become genuinely excellent at something:
- Going deep matters more than staying broad
- Domain expertise takes focused time
- Variety can hurt more than help
- Full-time employment might be better

### You Have Low Energy or Poor Time Management

If you struggle with:
- Managing your time
- Switching contexts
- Maintaining boundaries
- Consistent execution

**Fix those first.** Portfolio approach requires discipline.

## Practical Framework: Building Your Portfolio

### Step 1: Assess Your Current Situation

**What do you have now?**
- Employment?
- Clients?
- Products with revenue?
- Savings/runway?

**What do you need?**
- Stability?
- Higher income?
- Ownership?
- Flexibility?

**What are you missing?**
- Benefits?
- Upside potential?
- Colleagues?
- Creative freedom?

### Step 2: Choose Your Base

**Most stable options:**
1. Full-time employment
2. 2-3 retainer clients
3. Existing product with revenue

**Pick the one that:**
- Covers your expenses
- Provides benefits if you need them
- Gives you stability
- Doesn't consume all your energy

### Step 3: Add One Component

**Don't add multiple at once.** Pick one:

**If you have employment, add:**
- Products (ownership and upside)
- OR occasional consulting (extra income)

**If you have consulting, add:**
- Products (long-term asset)
- OR part-time employment (stability)

**If you have products with revenue, add:**
- Part-time employment (stability and colleagues)
- OR selective consulting (cash flow)

### Step 4: Test and Refine

**Give it 3-6 months:**
- Is this sustainable?
- Are you doing quality work in each area?
- Are you managing energy well?
- Is this better than single-track approach?

**Adjust:**
- If it's too much, drop something
- If it's working, stabilize it
- Maybe add one more component later
- Or keep it simple

### Step 5: Protect What's Working

**Once you have a working portfolio:**
- Maintain boundaries between components
- Don't let one consume everything
- Be ruthless about saying no
- Protect the balance

## What Success Looks Like

**You know your portfolio is working when:**

✅ You're not desperate about any single income source
✅ You can say no to bad opportunities
✅ Each component energizes different parts of you
✅ You're building both current income and future assets
✅ Losing one component wouldn't destroy you
✅ You're sustainable long-term
✅ You're making progress on multiple fronts
✅ You're not constantly exhausted

**You know it's not working when:**

❌ Everything feels chaotic
❌ You're doing all things poorly
❌ One component is consuming everything
❌ You're constantly exhausted
❌ Nothing is getting real attention
❌ You're overwhelmed, not energized
❌ Making no meaningful progress anywhere

## Frequently Asked Questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="How do you manage time across multiple income sources?">
    Ruthless scheduling. Employment gets defined hours. Products get specific blocks (e.g., Saturday mornings). Consulting is strictly limited (e.g., max 5 hours/month). Use calendar blocking, treat product time like meetings, and say no to anything that doesn't fit the structure.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can you really build a successful product part-time?">
    Yes, but it takes longer. Many successful products started as side projects: Craigslist, GitHub, Instagram were all side projects initially. The key: consistency (even 5 hours/week compounds) and patience (stable income means no rush to monetize).
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do you handle employer expectations about side projects?">
    Be transparent: discuss in interview that you build on the side. Choose companies that support this. Don't work on competing products. Do side work on your own time. Respect IP agreements. Deliver excellent work in your day job. Many employers are fine with this.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What if one component starts taking over everything?">
    Set hard boundaries. If employment is bleeding into product time, enforce work hours. If consulting is expanding, limit slots available. If products are all-consuming, schedule specific hours. The moment one thing dominates, the portfolio breaks down.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How much should each component contribute to income?">
    No fixed rule, but common splits: (1) Employment 80%, products 10%, consulting 10%. (2) Consulting 60%, products 30%, teaching 10%. (3) Employment 70%, products 20%, consulting 10%. The key: have one stable majority source, not equal splits that are all unstable.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="When should you drop a component from your portfolio?">
    When it's: (1) draining energy more than providing value, (2) preventing progress on more important things, (3) no longer serving its original purpose, or (4) making everything else worse. Portfolio should make life better, not more complex.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can you do employment + freelancing simultaneously?">
    Legally and practically, it depends on your employment contract. Some allow it, some don't. Check your contract for: moonlighting clauses, IP assignment, non-compete terms. If allowed, keep freelancing minimal so it doesn't affect day job performance.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How long does it take to build a working portfolio?">
    Start to stable portfolio: 1-2 years typically. Start with one base (6-12 months to stabilize), add one component (3-6 months to test), refine (3-6 months), maybe add another. Don't rush—building stable portfolio is better than building fast, unstable one.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Is portfolio approach just an excuse for not committing?">
    It can be, yes. If you're using it to avoid going deep on anything, that's a problem. But if you're deliberately balancing stability with upside, depth with breadth, and building resilience—that's strategy, not lack of commitment. Know which one you're doing.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What's the biggest advantage of portfolio approach?">
    Resilience. You're never desperate. You can walk away from bad situations. You can be patient with products. You can say no to wrong-fit opportunities. You build from abundance instead of desperation. That changes everything.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
